> **Important caveat on the (ij) vs (y) spelling.** The Dutch civil-registry form is **van Duijnhoven** (ij digraph). The USA branch (Johan's line) uses **van Duynhoven** (y). A Canadian using the **y** spelling most likely descends from a Dutch emigrant who anglicized the ij→y independently, exactly as Johan's family did — a shared spelling is *not* by itself proof of a shared ancestor. All Canadian leads below require civil-registry / immigration-record confirmation before being attached to the known tree.

## 4. Dutch → Canada emigration context (for record)

Dutch immigrants were the **5th-largest ethnic group** through Pier 21, Halifax (1928–1971). Key facts for locating a Canadian branch:
- **Pre-WWII (1918–1939):** ~15,000 Dutch settled, **mostly Ontario**.
- **Postwar (late 1940s–1950s):** the two provinces with the largest Dutch arrivals were **Ontario and Alberta**; interest in BC and other provinces grew through the 1950s. By the late 1950s arrivals were increasingly urban workers/professionals.
- **Major Dutch-Canadian communities** relevant to a search: Southern Ontario (Chatham-Kent, Holland Marsh), **Fraser Valley BC (Abbotsford)**, and Alberta Dutch Reformed communities.
- Johan's own family emigrated **NL→USA in Nov 1950** (Rotterdam→Harwich/Southampton), settling Green Isle, MN. If a sibling or cousin chose **Canada instead of the USA** in the same 1950s wave, Ontario or Alberta is the most probable landing point, arriving via **Pier 21, Halifax**.
