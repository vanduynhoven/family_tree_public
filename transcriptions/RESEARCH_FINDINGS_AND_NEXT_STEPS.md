## Surprising Discoveries

### 1. The Age Discrepancy Mystery

Anna Maria's U.S. naturalization certificate (Certificate No. 7468862) lists her birth year as **1918**, but her Dutch wedding booklet and passport clearly show **September 30, 1916**. This 2-year discrepancy was never corrected — she was naturalized as a U.S. citizen with an incorrect birth year on her official document.

**Significance:** This could cause complications for descendants researching records, as databases may show conflicting birth years.

### 3. The Rapid 6-Day Atlantic Crossing

The family's journey from the Netherlands to Minnesota took only **6 days** of travel:

```
November 17, 1950  Rotterdam, Netherlands (departure)
        ↓          Ferry across North Sea
November 17, 1950  Harwich, England (arrival)
        ↓          Train across England
November 17, 1950  Southampton, England
        ↓          Transatlantic ocean liner
November 23, 1950  New York Harbor, USA (arrival)
        ↓          Train/bus to Minnesota
Late Nov 1950      Green Isle, Minnesota (final destination)
```

This was remarkably efficient for 1950s emigration. The Rotterdam-Harwich-Southampton route was a common "emigrant corridor" at the time.

### 4. The 1906 Marriage Record Reveals a Generation of Deaths

The marriage certificate of Johan's parents (Marianus and Anna Maria, 1906) lists all 12 children born to Marianus's parents — Johan's **grandparents**. Several died young:

- **Two daughters named Johanna** — one died at age 4, another born later died at age 28
- This suggests the family **reused names after infant deaths**, a common Dutch Catholic practice
- Multiple children marked as "overleden" (deceased) by the time of the 1906 wedding

### 5. Same-Day Naturalization for the Entire Family

On **June 5, 1956**, the entire Van Duynhoven family became U.S. citizens simultaneously:

| Person | Age at Naturalization | Certificate Number |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Johan van Duynhoven | 40 | 7468861 |
| Anna Maria van Duynhoven | 39 | 7468862 |
| Morris John | 5 | (derivative) |
| Wilhelmus Marinus | 3 | (derivative) |
| Anna Maria | 2 | (derivative) |
| Peter John | 4 months | (derivative) |

The children were naturalized "derivatively" through their parents — they didn't need separate certificates. The consecutive certificate numbers (7468861 and 7468862) show Johan and Anna were processed together.

### 6. Dutch Citizenship Was Irrevocably Lost

Under the **1892 Dutch Nationality Law (Wet op het Nederlanderschap en Ingezetenschap, WNI)**, specifically **Artikel 7, lid 1**:

> *"Het Nederlanderschap gaat verloren door naturalisatie in een vreemd land."*
> 
> (Dutch nationality is lost through naturalization in a foreign country.)

**What this means:**
- The moment Johan and Anna took the U.S. oath of citizenship on June 5, 1956, they **automatically and permanently lost** Dutch citizenship
- Their four American-born children had acquired Dutch citizenship at birth through Johan (jus sanguinis)
- The children also **lost Dutch citizenship** when their parents naturalized — at ages 5, 3, 2, and 4 months
- No option existed to retain dual nationality under Dutch law at that time
- This loss is **irrevocable** — modern Dutch citizenship laws do not provide a path to reclaim citizenship lost under the 1892 law

### 7. Unusual Spelling Variations in the Family Name

The family name appears in multiple spellings across documents:

| Spelling | Where Used |
|----------|------------|
| **van Duynhoven** | U.S. naturalization certificates, immigration visa |
| **van Duijnhoven** | Dutch wedding booklet, some church records |
| **Van Duynhoven** | Americanized capitalization |

The "ij" (a Dutch digraph called "lange ij" or "Griekse ij") was often simplified to "y" in American documents because:
- English typewriters lacked the ij character
- Immigration officials wrote what they heard phonetically
- The family may have adopted the "y" spelling to ease American pronunciation

Both spellings are considered correct and refer to the same family.

## Most Valuable Next Steps

### 1. Request the NARA "A-File" (Highest Priority)

The **Alien Registration File (A-file)** for Johan and Anna would contain:
- Original visa applications with detailed personal history
- Sponsor affidavits (who sponsored their immigration)
- Photographs from the visa application
- Medical examination records
- Employment and financial information
- Correspondence about their case

**How to request:**
1. Go to [archives.gov/research/immigration](https://www.archives.gov/research/immigration)
2. File a **Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)** request
3. Include: Full names, birth dates, naturalization certificate numbers (7468861, 7468862), naturalization date (June 5, 1956)
4. Processing time: 6-12 months typical

### 2. Search Ship Manifests on FamilySearch

The November 1950 passenger manifest for the ship from Southampton to New York would list:
- Exact ship name
- All family members traveling together
- Last residence in Netherlands
- Final destination in USA
- Contact person in America (sponsor)

**Search:** [familysearch.org](https://www.familysearch.org) → Immigration & Travel → New York Passenger Arrival Lists

### 3. Order Records from BHIC

The Brabants Historisch Informatie Centrum has digitized most civil registration from Noord-Brabant. You can:
- Search free online for index entries
- Order certified copies of original documents
- Access extended family records (siblings, cousins, grandparents)

**Especially valuable:** Birth certificates often include marginalia (side notes) recording marriages and deaths, creating a timeline for each person.
